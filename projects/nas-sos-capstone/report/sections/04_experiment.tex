% ---- The text from here to the end of this subsection was AI-drafted on

% on the print ("Pare down."). To be checked against

For aircraft $i$ the cost of an option is its extra fuel plus its cost index times its extra time, in kilograms of fuel, the form used in \cite{mori2022massCruise}:
\begin{equation}
c_i = \Delta m_{f,i} + \mathit{CI}_i\, \Delta t_i ,
\label{eq:ac-cost}
\end{equation}
where the extra fuel $\Delta m_{f,i}$ and the extra time $\Delta t_i$ are the option minus the baseline of flying on undisturbed, and $\mathit{CI}_i$ is the cost index of aircraft $i$ in kilograms of fuel per minute.

% The decider's equation (J, the lowest estimate over all aircraft) is removed as
% the owner marked on the 2026-10-13 print, page 20 ("Remove"). The words "the
% option whose estimated total of Eq. 1 over all aircraft is lowest" are Claude's,
% added so the paragraph still says how an automated case chooses.
Case A applies its rule, one descent. Each automated case chooses, among the options it is allowed to offer, the option whose estimated total of Eq.~\ref{eq:ac-cost} over all aircraft is lowest. Case B uses the type's nominal mass for every aircraft and values time at 1 kg/min. Cases C and D use each aircraft's actual mass and cost index. Case D also rejects an option that delays a flight with connections past its threshold. Whatever a case chooses is then scored at the actual masses, and that score is what the tables report.

Schedule reliability enters as a step: a flight with connections that is delayed past its threshold incurs a cost $K$ on top of Eq.~\ref{eq:ac-cost}. No sourced value of $K$ is held, so the tables report whether the threshold is crossed, not a dollar figure. Carbon dioxide is 3.16 kg per kilogram of fuel \cite{icao2024icecMethodology}. Instructions are counted, now and later, and are not converted to anything.

Drag, maximum thrust and fuel flow are taken from OpenAP \cite{sun2020openap}, which uses a two-term drag polar and, above 30,000 ft, the two-shaft turbofan thrust model of \textcite{bartel2008thrustFuel}. A descent is flown at 1,500 ft/min. A climb is flown at maximum thrust, at the rate that thrust allows and no more than 1,500 ft/min. A level is available to an aircraft only if it would still have 150 ft/min of climb left there. Cases A and B do not know the mass and so cannot make that test; they offer a climb only at or below FL350, which the scenario does not explore. Cases C and D make it at the aircraft's actual mass.

Figure~\ref{fig:envelope} is drawn assuming lift equals weight. Its low-speed boundary is 1.3 times the stall speed at an assumed clean maximum lift coefficient, and its high-speed boundary is the more restrictive of the maximum operating Mach number and the maximum operating speed (VMO). The aircraft's present level and Mach number are marked, and each altitude and speed instruction the scenario offers it is marked as inside or outside the region with 150 ft/min of climb left.

The model makes the following simplifications.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Standard atmosphere, no wind, lift equal to weight.} Time differences come only from true airspeed and added track distance.
\item \textbf{No compressibility drag.} The drag used here has no Mach-number term. The OpenAP paper does include one, a wave-drag term above a critical Mach number, and says the effect ``should not be ignored'' there \cite{sun2020openap}; the installed package leaves the term off unless asked and marks it experimental, and the experiment runs with it off. The FAA classes flight from Mach 0.75 to 1.20 as transonic \cite{faa2015ac61107b}, so the scenario's Mach 0.80 is at the low end of that range. With OpenAP's experimental wave-drag term switched on, drag at Mach 0.80 is 2 to 4 percent higher. That is 1.3 kN for the heavy aircraft at FL380, against 2.7 kN of excess thrust, so its climb left falls from 181 to 93 ft/min, under the 100 ft/min of the service ceiling. The light aircraft at FL400 falls from 322 to 239 ft/min and still clears 150. The weights in the scenario were set with the term off. Not decided: whether to switch the term on and set the weights again.
\item \textbf{The drag polar is the 737 MAX 8's.} OpenAP has none for the 737 MAX 9.
\item \textbf{The fuel curve is the package default.} OpenAP has no fuel model fitted to the 737 MAX 9 or MAX 8; it uses its default coefficients scaled by the LEAP-1B take-off fuel flow. The installed version of the package (2.6.2) uses a different curve from the one the OpenAP paper describes, so the paper is the source of the fuel-flow data (the ICAO engine emissions databank) and the package is the source of the curve.
\item \textbf{The climb margins.} The service ceiling is the level with 100 ft/min of climb left \cite{faa2015ac61107b}. The 150 ft/min the experiment requires is commonly observed in practice.
\item \textbf{One climb rate per climb.} The rate is taken at the mid-altitude and held; the climb is not integrated. A climb is not checked against the time left before the crossing.
\item \textbf{Mass is constant.} Fuel burned during the manoeuvre does not reduce the mass.
\item \textbf{The low-speed limit rests on an assumed maximum lift coefficient} ($C_{L\max} = 1.45$) and is a stall margin, not a buffet boundary.
\item \textbf{A speed change on the crossing pair is not calculated.} It is marked unable to separate the pair by construction.
\end{enumerate}
