\section{Assumptions and Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

The problem is: the inertia of past, piecemeal designs and the high cost of upgrading 
the NAS make it hard to change the system. The project proposes a new airline-to-ATC 
interface and a new way of deciding which aircraft to move in a conflict, that together 
have the ability to lower the cost of air traffic management. 

Towards those ends, the projet employs a model-based systems engineering (MBSE) approach 
to design the architecture and the decision-making process, and a simulation-based experiment 
to test the proposed interface and decision-making process. First, a literature review was conducted to: 
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Identify the current state of air traffic management,
    \item Identify the relevant stakeholders and their objectives,
    \item Identify the requirements that are necessary to meet those objectives, and
    \item Identify the gaps in the current system that the proposed interface and decision-making process can address.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Systems Engineering Approach}
\label{sec:se-approach}

Standard systems engineering methods of systems analysis and decomposition were used on the collected evidence, including 
generating a context diagram, internal block diagrams, and activity and sequence views. 
These methods were used to identify the relevant systems and the interactions amongst them 
that are necessary to meet the stakeholders' objectives. These were 
used to generate the stakeholder requirements that are necessary to meet those objectives, which were
then decomposed into NAS systems-level requirements. The NAS SLRs were then allocated to the 
relevant sub-systems and HLRs decomposed those one level further. 

This enabled the project to:
(1) build a model of the architecture and the decision-making process, 
(2) Compare the current air traffic management practice, which leverages
general rules of thumb and knowledge of successful historical actions \cite{ref:TBD_TODO}, with a 
model-based decision support approach that replaces the low-fidelity decision rule with one or more 
higher-fidelity models of aircraft performance data of varying levels of accuracy.

The working assumptions can be categorized in two ways: 
(1) assumptions about the problem and its context, and 
(2) assumptions about the methodology.
The problem statement assumes that:
\begin{itemize}
    \item the abstractions used in the model are sufficient to capture the relevant behavior of the system
    \item the stakeholders' objectives are comprehensively captured and accurately represented in the model
\end{itemize}
Every abstraction was done with the expressed goal of capturing the relevant behavior of the system, as defined
by the stakeholders' objectives. The decision was made to ratify the NAS-as-SoS System of Interest around the 
trajectory-intent lifecycle from enterprise objectives through aircraft motion. An entity is modeled when it 
has distinct decision authority or execution
behavior dynamically coupled to in-scope decisions and materially affects cost, safety,
workload, schedule reliability, or passenger value. Boundary actors supply objectives,
demand, feedback, or rules without being decomposed as decision mechanisms. Exogenous
influences enter as constraints or scenario parameters. This criterion preserves the
authority and responsibility relationships needed for a systems-of-systems architecture
without expanding the project into a model of every NAS participant.

The methodology does not assume that the proposed methods are better, which is tested
as part of the experiment. Instead, it assumes that:
\begin{itemize}
\item models of every required aircraft type are available
\item the security concerns of sharing airline data can be mitigated
\item the proposed interface can be implemented in the NAS
\item digital communication channels exist between the airline and ATC
\item the specific scenario presented in the experiment is representative of 
a larger set of scenarios which generalize to the NAS as a whole
\end{itemize}

An emphasis is put on structure/behavior/interfaces/traceability rather than optimization math, 
as the project's commercial value for the author lies in it furthering their understanding of
the systems, their interactions, and the complex series of responsibility divisions that 
inhibit information flow and lead to sub-optimal outcomes. In order to explore this concept
in a meaningful way, decision-support/optimization is treated as a capability inside the
architecture, which is enabled by following MBSE principles. 

While more detailed documentations of the systems and their constituent systems are possible 
(with examples contained in \cite{appendix_what_TODO}), 
and more formal mathematical models can be employed, such an effort was deemed outside the 
scope of the work required to achieve the commercial value for the customer. In true customer-focused
MBSE fasion, the project's artifacts are tailored to the customer's needs. 

As such, individual steps in the process were tailored to the availability of the author, including 
a continuous, ad-hoc review of relevant literature, after an initial review was conducted. A full 
gate-to-gate conops was unofficially constructed and refined with publicly available general 
information/knowledge of the NAS. No alternative system decompositions were formall explored
past initial abstraction decisiosn. In this way, the ISO/IEC/IEEE 15288 technical processes
were followed, which includes tailoring them to the application.

\subsubsection{Equations of motion}

Each aircraft is a point mass in steady flight in still air in the International Standard Atmosphere. OpenAP \cite{sun2020openap} uses the point-mass equations along and normal to the flight path,
\begin{align}
T - D - m g \sin\gamma &= m \frac{dV}{dt}, \label{eq:eom-along}\\
L &= m g \cos\gamma, \label{eq:eom-normal}
\end{align}
where $T$ is total thrust, $D$ drag, $L$ lift, $m$ mass, $V$ true airspeed and $\gamma$ the flight path angle, with $\tan\gamma = \dot h / V$ for a vertical speed $\dot h$. The experiment sets $dV/dt = 0$ everywhere: every aircraft holds its Mach number. In level cruise $\gamma = 0$, so $T = D$ and $L = mg$.

True airspeed comes from the Mach number $M$ and the standard temperature $\Theta(h)$ at the altitude $h$:
\begin{equation}
V = M \sqrt{\kappa R\, \Theta(h)}, \qquad
\Theta(h) = \begin{cases} 288.15 - 0.0019812\,h & h < 36{,}089 \text{ ft} \\ 216.65 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}
\label{eq:tas}
\end{equation}
with $\kappa = 1.4$, $R = 287.05$ J/(kg K), $h$ in feet and $\Theta$ in kelvin.

\subsubsection{Drag, thrust and fuel flow (OpenAP)}

\textbf{Drag.} OpenAP computes clean-configuration drag from a two-term drag polar:
\begin{equation}
C_L = \frac{m g \cos\gamma}{\tfrac{1}{2}\rho V^2 S}, \qquad
C_D = C_{D0} + k\, C_L^2, \qquad
D = \tfrac{1}{2}\rho V^2 S\, C_D .
\label{eq:drag}
\end{equation}
The values used are in Table~\ref{tab:openap-params}. This is where aircraft mass enters: a heavier aircraft needs a higher $C_L$ and its drag rises with $C_L^2$.

\textbf{Assumption: no compressibility drag.} Eq.~\ref{eq:drag} has no Mach-number term, so the extra drag from shock waves on the wing is left out. The FAA circular classes flight below Mach 0.75 as subsonic and Mach 0.75 to 1.20 as transonic \cite{faa2015ac61107b} (para.~1-4e), so the scenario's Mach 0.80 is at the low end of the transonic range. OpenAP has an optional wave-drag term, off by default and marked experimental by its authors. Switching it on is the only estimate of the size of this assumption held so far: at the scenario's weights and levels, drag rises by 2 to 4 percent at Mach 0.80 and by less than 1 percent at Mach 0.76. Fuel flow in level cruise moves by about the same few percent. The rate of climb left (Eq.~\ref{eq:roc}) moves much more, because it is a small difference between thrust and drag; see Section~\ref{sec:scoring-limits}.

\textbf{Maximum thrust.} Above 30,000 ft OpenAP uses the high-altitude segment of the two-shaft turbofan model of Bartel and Young \cite{bartel2008thrustFuel}, which scales a reference thrust at the top of climb by pressure and Mach number:
\begin{equation}
T_{\max} = T_{cr} \left[ d \,\ln\!\frac{p}{p_{cr}} + \left(\frac{M}{M_{cr}}\right)^{-0.11} \right], \qquad
d = -0.4204\,\frac{M}{M_{cr}} + 1.0824 ,
\label{eq:thrust}
\end{equation}
where $p$ is the ambient pressure, and $p_{cr}$ and $M_{cr}$ are the pressure and Mach number at the type's reference cruise condition. The package returns the same value for its cruise and climb thrust at these altitudes, so there is one thrust limit, not two.

\textbf{Fuel flow.} OpenAP finds the thrust the manoeuvre needs from Eq.~\ref{eq:eom-along},
\begin{equation}
T_{req} = D + m g \sin\gamma ,
\label{eq:treq}
\end{equation}
and converts it to fuel flow with an empirical curve of thrust ratio, scaled by the engine's take-off fuel flow from the ICAO engine emissions databank:
\begin{equation}
\dot m_f = n\, s \left[ c_1 - \exp\!\left( -c_2 \left( x\, e^{c_3 x} - \frac{\ln c_1}{c_2} \right) \right) \right], \qquad
x = \frac{T_{req}}{n\, T_{0}} ,
\label{eq:fuelflow}
\end{equation}
where $n$ is the number of engines, $T_0$ the sea-level static thrust of one engine, $s$ the engine's take-off fuel flow, and $x$ is limited smoothly to between 0.03 and 1.2. In level cruise $T_{req} = D$, so fuel flow depends on mass only through the drag of Eq.~\ref{eq:drag}.

\subsubsection{Whether an aircraft can be cleared to a level}

The rate of climb an aircraft has left in level flight at a mass, Mach number and altitude is its excess thrust times its speed, divided by its weight:
\begin{equation}
RC = \frac{\left( T_{\max} - D \right) V}{m g} .
\label{eq:roc}
\end{equation}
This is Eq.~\ref{eq:eom-along} with $dV/dt = 0$, solved for $\dot h = V \sin\gamma$. A level is available to an aircraft only if
\begin{equation}
RC(m, M, h) \ge RC_{\min} .
\label{eq:feasible}
\end{equation}
$RC = 0$ is the level above which the aircraft cannot hold altitude. $RC = 100$ ft/min is the definition of the service ceiling \cite{faa2015ac61107b}. The experiment uses $RC_{\min} = 150$ ft/min, the margin the author has seen airlines keep for clear air turbulence and other unknowns (industry experience, no document held; D-023). The envelope figure (Figure~\ref{fig:envelope}) draws all three lines for the heavy aircraft.

The low-speed side of that figure is not from OpenAP. It is 1.3 times the speed at which lift equals weight at an assumed maximum lift coefficient $C_{L\max} = 1.45$:
\begin{equation}
V_{\min} = 1.3 \sqrt{\frac{2 m g}{\rho S\, C_{L\max}}} .
\label{eq:vmin}
\end{equation}
The high-speed side is the lower of the maximum operating Mach number (0.82) and the maximum operating calibrated airspeed (340 kt).

Which mass goes into Eq.~\ref{eq:feasible} is the experiment. Cases A and B do not know the mass, so they do not evaluate it: they offer a climb only at or below FL350. Cases C and D evaluate it at the aircraft's actual mass. The scoring then checks every chosen option again at the actual mass; an option the aircraft cannot fly is reported as ``unable''.

\subsubsection{Cost of one manoeuvre}

Every cost is a difference from flying on undisturbed. Write $\dot m_f(h, \dot h)$ for the fuel flow of Eq.~\ref{eq:fuelflow} at the aircraft's mass and Mach number, $h_0$ for the present level, $h_1$ for the new one, $t_h$ for the time the manoeuvre is held (15 min) and $\dot m_{f0} = \dot m_f(h_0, 0)$.

\textbf{Altitude change.} A descent is flown at 1,500 ft/min. A climb is flown at maximum thrust, at the rate that thrust allows: Eq.~\ref{eq:roc} evaluated at the mid-altitude, and no more than 1,500 ft/min (D-024):
\begin{equation}
\dot h = \begin{cases} -1{,}500 \text{ ft/min} & \text{descent} \\ \min\!\left( 1{,}500,\; RC\!\left(m, M, \tfrac{h_0+h_1}{2}\right) \right) & \text{climb} \end{cases}
\qquad t_c = \frac{|h_1 - h_0|}{|\dot h|} .
\label{eq:climb-rate}
\end{equation}
At the scenario's weights and levels the climb rate is 250 to 500 ft/min, so a 2,000 ft climb takes four to eight minutes. Fuel flow during the change is evaluated at the mid-altitude:
\begin{equation}
\Delta m_f = \underbrace{\left[ \dot m_f\!\left(\tfrac{h_0+h_1}{2}, \dot h\right) - \dot m_{f0} \right] t_c}_{\text{the level change}}
+ \underbrace{\left[ \dot m_f(h_1, 0) - \dot m_{f0} \right] t_h}_{\text{the hold at the new level}}
+ \underbrace{\left[ \dot m_f\!\left(\tfrac{h_0+h_1}{2}, \dot h_b\right) - \dot m_{f0} \right] t_b}_{\text{climb back, after a descent only}} .
\label{eq:alt-fuel}
\end{equation}
Here $\dot h_b$ and $t_b$ are the rate and time of the climb back to $h_0$, from Eq.~\ref{eq:climb-rate}. The time difference is the same ground distance flown at a different true airspeed,
\begin{equation}
\Delta t = t_h \left( \frac{V(h_0)}{V(h_1)} - 1 \right),
\label{eq:alt-time}
\end{equation}
which is zero between levels above 36,089 ft, where the standard temperature is constant. The return to the original level after a climb is counted as an instruction but given no fuel term.

\textbf{Vector.} A pass-behind vector adds a fixed track distance $d_x$ (6 NM) at the cruise speed and fuel flow:
\begin{equation}
\Delta t = \frac{d_x}{V(h_0)}, \qquad \Delta m_f = \dot m_{f0}\, \Delta t .
\label{eq:vector}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Slow-down} (the in-trail aircraft in option O9). The aircraft flies at $M - \Delta M$ the distance it would have covered in $t_s$ minutes at $M$:
\begin{equation}
t_s' = t_s \frac{V(M)}{V(M - \Delta M)}, \qquad
\Delta t = t_s' - t_s, \qquad
\Delta m_f = \dot m_f(M - \Delta M)\, t_s' - \dot m_{f0}\, t_s .
\label{eq:slow}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Speed change on the crossing pair.} Not calculated. It is marked unable to separate the pair by construction (D-014).

\subsubsection{From manoeuvre cost to the outcome table}

For aircraft $i$ the cost of an option is extra fuel plus the cost index times extra time, in kilograms of fuel, the form used in \cite{mori2022massCruise}:
\begin{equation}
c_i = \Delta m_{f,i} + CI_i\, \Delta t_i .
\label{eq:ac-cost}
\end{equation}
Each case chooses, among the options it is allowed to offer, the one with the lowest estimate
\begin{equation}
J = \sum_i \left( \Delta m_{f,i}(\hat m_i) + \widehat{CI}_i\, \Delta t_i(\hat m_i) \right),
\label{eq:decider}
\end{equation}
where the hats mark what the case believes. Case A does not use Eq.~\ref{eq:decider}; it applies its rule (one descent). Case B uses the type's nominal mass for every aircraft and values time at 1 kg/min. Cases C and D use each aircraft's actual mass and cost index. Case D also rejects an option that pushes a flight with connections past the connection threshold. Whatever a case chooses is then scored at the actual masses, and that score is what the tables report.

The other columns follow from Eq.~\ref{eq:alt-fuel} to \ref{eq:ac-cost}. Carbon dioxide is $3.16\, \Delta m_f$ \cite{icao2024icecMethodology}. An airline's cost in dollars is the sum over its aircraft of $c_i\, p_f + 3.16\, \Delta m_{f,i}\, p_c$, with $p_f$ the fuel price and $p_c$ the carbon price. System fuel is $\sum_i \Delta m_{f,i}$. Instructions are counted, now and later, and are not converted to anything.

\subsubsection{Worked example: option O4, the light aircraft climbs 2,000 ft}

The light aircraft is at 62,980 kg, FL380, Mach 0.80. Eq.~\ref{eq:roc} gives 322 ft/min left at FL400, above 150, so the level is available to a case that knows the mass. Eq.~\ref{eq:climb-rate} gives a climb rate of 409 ft/min at FL390, so the climb takes $2{,}000 / 409$ min, or 294 s. Fuel flows from Eq.~\ref{eq:fuelflow}: 0.5572 kg/s at FL380 (2,006 kg/h), 0.5465 kg/s at FL400 (1,968 kg/h), and 0.6321 kg/s in the climb. Eq.~\ref{eq:alt-fuel} gives
\begin{equation*}
\Delta m_f = (0.6321 - 0.5572)(294) + (0.5465 - 0.5572)(900) = 22.0 - 9.6 = 12.4 \text{ kg},
\end{equation*}
and $\Delta t = 0$ because both levels are above 36,089 ft. This is the 12.4 kg in the options table.

\subsubsection{Limitations found in writing this up}
\label{sec:scoring-limits}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{No compressibility drag, and the climb left is sensitive to it.} With OpenAP's experimental wave-drag term switched on, drag at Mach 0.80 is 2 to 4 percent higher. That is 1.3 kN for the heavy aircraft at FL380, against 2.7 kN of excess thrust, so its climb left falls from 181 to 93 ft/min, under the 100 ft/min of the service ceiling. The light aircraft at FL400 falls from 322 to 239 ft/min and still clears 150. The weights in the scenario were set with the term off. Not decided: whether to switch the term on and set the weights again.
\item \textbf{One climb rate per climb.} The rate is taken at the mid-altitude and held; the climb is not integrated. A climb is not checked against the time left before the crossing (12 min); the longest in the base scenario is the heavy aircraft's 4,000 ft climb back after option O8, about 12 min, which happens after the conflict.
\item \textbf{The fuel curve is the package default.} OpenAP has no fuel model fitted to the 737 MAX 9 or MAX 8; Eq.~\ref{eq:fuelflow} uses its default coefficients scaled by the LEAP-1B take-off fuel flow.
\item \textbf{The drag polar is the 737 MAX 8's} (D-017).
\item \textbf{Mass is constant.} Fuel burned during the manoeuvre does not reduce the mass.
\item \textbf{The low-speed limit rests on an assumed $C_{L\max}$} and is a stall margin, not a buffet boundary.
\item \textbf{Standard atmosphere, no wind}; time differences come only from true airspeed and added track distance.
\end{enumerate}
