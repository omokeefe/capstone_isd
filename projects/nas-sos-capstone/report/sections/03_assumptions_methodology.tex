The problem is that the inertia of past, piecemeal designs and the high cost of upgrading the NAS make the system hard to change. The project proposes a new airline-to-ATC interface and a new way of deciding which aircraft to move in a conflict, which together could lower the cost of air traffic management.

Toward those ends, the project uses a model-based systems engineering (MBSE) approach to design the architecture and the decision-making process, and a simulation-based experiment to test the proposed interface and decision-making process. First, a literature review was conducted to:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item identify the current state of air traffic management,
    \item identify the relevant stakeholders and their objectives,
    \item identify the requirements necessary to meet those objectives, and
    \item identify the gaps in the current system that the proposed interface and decision-making process can address.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Systems Engineering Approach}
\label{sec:se-approach}

Standard systems engineering methods of analysis and decomposition were applied to the collected evidence, producing a context diagram, internal block diagrams, and activity and sequence views. These identified the relevant systems and the interactions among them that are necessary to meet the stakeholders' objectives. From them the stakeholder requirements were written and then decomposed into system-level requirements (SLRs) for the NAS. The SLRs were allocated to the relevant constituent systems, and high-level requirements (HLRs) decomposed them one level further.

This enabled the project to (1) build a model of the architecture and the decision-making process, and (2) compare current air traffic management practice, which relies on general rules of thumb and knowledge of what has worked before~\cite{rantanen2012conflictManeuvers,kirwan2001coraStrategies}, with a model-based decision-support approach that replaces the low-fidelity decision rule with one or more higher-fidelity models of aircraft performance, at varying levels of accuracy.

The emphasis is on structure, behavior, interfaces, and traceability, not on optimization mathematics. The project's commercial value to the author lies in a better understanding of the systems, their interactions, and the divisions of responsibility that inhibit information flow and lead to sub-optimal outcomes. To explore that in a meaningful way, decision support and optimization are treated as a capability inside the architecture, which following MBSE principles makes possible.

More detailed documentation of the systems and their constituent systems is possible (examples are in Appendix~\ref{app:architecture}), and more formal mathematical models could be used. That effort was judged to be outside the work required to deliver the commercial value to the customer. In keeping with customer-focused MBSE, the project's artifacts are tailored to the customer's needs.

Individual steps in the process were likewise tailored to the author's availability. After an initial literature review, the review continued ad hoc as the work required. A full gate-to-gate concept of operations was constructed informally and refined with publicly available general knowledge of the NAS. No alternative system decompositions were formally explored beyond the initial abstraction decisions. In this way the ISO/IEC/IEEE 15288 technical processes were followed, including the tailoring of those processes to the application that the standard provides for.

\subsection{Working Assumptions}
\label{sec:assumptions}

The working assumptions fall into two groups: assumptions about the problem and its context, and assumptions about the methodology.

The problem statement assumes that:
\begin{itemize}
    \item the abstractions used in the model are sufficient to capture the relevant behavior of the system;
    \item the stakeholders' objectives are comprehensively captured and accurately represented in the model.
\end{itemize}

Every abstraction was made with the express goal of capturing the relevant behavior of the system, as defined by the stakeholders' objectives. The System of Interest, the NAS as a system of systems, was ratified around the trajectory-intent lifecycle, from enterprise objectives through aircraft motion. An entity is modeled when it has distinct decision authority or execution behavior that is dynamically coupled to in-scope decisions and materially affects cost, safety, workload, schedule reliability, or passenger value. Boundary actors supply objectives, demand, feedback, or rules without being decomposed as decision mechanisms. Exogenous influences enter as constraints or scenario parameters. This criterion preserves the authority and responsibility relationships a system-of-systems architecture needs without expanding the project into a model of every NAS participant.

The methodology does not assume that the proposed methods are better; that is what the experiment tests. It assumes that:
\begin{itemize}
    \item models of every required aircraft type are available;
    \item the security concerns of sharing airline data can be mitigated;
    \item the proposed interface can be implemented in the NAS;
    \item digital communication channels exist between the airline and ATC;
    \item the specific scenario presented in the experiment is representative of a larger set of scenarios that generalize to the NAS as a whole.
\end{itemize}

The last of these assumptions carries the most weight, and the following limits bound it:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Conflicts between two aircraft crossing at the same level are a minority of conflicts: 36 of 256 in the operational data of \textcite{rantanen2012conflictManeuvers}.
    \item The experiment uses one invented en-route sector and four aircraft.
    \item The conflict is a static snapshot; the traffic does not evolve while the decision is made.
    \item The rule that limits climbs when the weight is unknown is a single threshold (FL350) applied to every aircraft type.
    \item The airline is assumed to report its weight and cost index truthfully. An airline could misreport what it shares.
\end{itemize}
The simplifications inside the aircraft performance model are listed separately in Section~\ref{sec:scoring-limits}.

The items the interface carries are not new. \textcite{coppenbarger1999climbPrediction} listed take-off weight, thrust and drag factors, and the speed profile as candidates for exchange between an airline and air traffic control, and measured what airline-supplied weight does for the prediction of a climb. In Europe, the Extended Projected Profile sent over ADS-C downlinks the trajectory the flight management system predicts~\cite{sesarju2019solutionsCatalogue}. What has kept the items out of use is ownership, not feasibility: weight and speed intent ``are considered competitive parameters by airlines and are not available for use''~\cite{schultz2012adaptiveClimb}.

The proposal here keeps the items and changes the receiver and the purpose. In the earlier work the data feeds a trajectory predictor, to make the prediction of one aircraft's path more accurate. Here it feeds the decision support of the party that chooses which aircraft to move, to change the choice. The interface is typed in the model, tagged as part of the improved architecture, and tied to the requirement it satisfies, so the report can point at a model element where the earlier work has a finding in prose. Confidentiality is stated as an assumption: the data is disclosed only to the deciding party, and no security mechanism is modeled.
