\section{Assumptions and Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

The project uses a model-based systems engineering (MBSE) approach to define the architecture and the decision-making process, and a simulation-based experiment to test the proposed updates to them. First, a literature review was conducted to:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item identify the current state of air traffic management,
    \item identify the relevant stakeholders and their objectives,
    \item identify the requirements necessary to meet those objectives, and
    \item identify the gaps in the current system that the proposed interface and decision-making process can address.
\end{enumerate}
The contextual information captured in the literature review enabled the execution of the systems engineering approach.

\subsection{Systems Engineering Approach}
\label{sec:se-approach}

The systems engineering approach follows the technical processes of ISO/IEC/IEEE 15288, tailored to the study as the standard provides for, in the eight steps of Table~\ref{tab:se-processes}; each later section names the step it covers. Stakeholder needs and requirements definition came first (step 1). From the literature survey and the author's experience, a PESTLE analysis produced the candidate stakeholders, an assessment of their power and interest kept four of them, and an exploration of their interests produced twelve candidate objectives, six of which bear on the decision studied. Business or mission analysis and system requirements definition then identified the constituent systems through which those stakeholders act, and drew the boundary of the System of Interest around them in a context diagram (step 2). Stakeholder requirements were written against the kept objectives and decomposed into system-level requirements (SLRs) for the NAS; the SLRs were allocated to the constituent systems, and high-level requirements (HLRs) decomposed them one level further (step 3). Architecture definition modeled the structure of the constituent systems in block definition and internal block diagrams (step 4), and their behavior in the use case, the nominal flight, and the conflict-resolution activity and sequence (step 5). Assessing that architecture against the requirements found the gap: a requirement that no exchange in today's architecture can meet (step 6). Design definition set out the ways the model's own breakdown of the decision offers to close the gap and, drawing on the literature and the author's experience, chose one of them, the airline-to-ATC interface (step 7). Finally, system analysis, verification and validation ran the experiment at three fidelities, verified each requirement on the studied thread for each means of meeting it, and read the results back to the stakeholders' objectives (step 8).
% ---- end AI draft

This enabled the project to (1) build a model of the decision-making process, and (2) compare current air traffic management practice, which relies on general rules of thumb and knowledge of what has worked before~\cite{rantanen2012conflictManeuvers,kirwan2001coraStrategies}, with a model-based decision-support approach that replaces the low-fidelity decision rule with one or more higher-fidelity models of aircraft performance, at varying levels of accuracy and information.

The emphasis is on structure, behavior, interfaces, and traceability, not on optimization mathematics.

\subsection{Working Assumptions}
\label{sec:assumptions}

\aidraft{Restructured 2026-11-14 from the owner's markup of the 2026-11-10 print. The opening is made more fluid and states the systems engineering assumptions in the owner's margin words; the abstraction paragraph now starts from the owner's note (the models began from first principles and were abstracted afterwards) and rests on the first list; the methodology list carries the owner's edits, and the controllers' communication assumption moved into it from the paragraph below; the paragraphs after the list add detail only, and say where each assumption sits on the thread. The working assumptions fall into two groups: those the systems engineering process makes as it frames the problem, and those the methodology makes as it tests the proposed solution. The problem is that the NAS is hard to change, and that a new airline-to-ATC interface and a new way of deciding which aircraft to move in a conflict could lower the cost of air traffic management. The systems engineering process that frames it assumes that each stage produces an enriched, comprehensive understanding of the system at the highest level, and that the decisions made about abstraction capture reality sufficiently. In particular, it assumes that:
\begin{itemize}
    \item the abstractions used in the model are sufficient to capture the relevant behavior of the system;
    \item the stakeholders' objectives are comprehensively captured and accurately represented in the model;
    \item the scenario is sufficient to explore the potential benefit of interconnected MBSE.
\end{itemize}

The models produced began from first principles, informed by the literature survey, and were subsequently abstracted where the boundary of the System of Interest made it necessary. This rests on the first two assumptions above: every abstraction was made with the express goal of capturing the relevant behavior of the system, as defined by the stakeholders' objectives. The System of Interest, the NAS as a system of systems, was ratified around the trajectory-intent lifecycle, from enterprise objectives through aircraft motion. An entity is modeled when it has distinct decision authority or execution behavior that is dynamically coupled to in-scope decisions and materially affects cost, safety, workload, schedule reliability, or passenger value. Boundary actors supply objectives, demand, feedback, or rules without being decomposed as decision mechanisms. Exogenous influences enter as constraints or scenario parameters. This criterion preserves the authority and responsibility relationships a system-of-systems architecture needs without expanding the project into a model of every NAS participant.

The methodology is a model-based systems engineering approach that uses simulation to test the proposed interface and decision-making process. It assumes that:
\begin{itemize}
    \item models of every required aircraft type are available, to attain the ``truth'' every case is scored against;
    \item the security concerns of sharing airline data can be mitigated;
    \item the proposed interface can be implemented;
    \item the airline reports its aircraft performance (fuel, weight, drag and fuel flow) accurately;
    \item the controllers can communicate with the aircraft and the airline, and can issue instructions to the aircraft in a timely manner;
    \item the specific scenario presented in the experiment is representative of a larger set of scenarios that generalize to the NAS as a whole;
    \item wake turbulence is not modeled, so the aircraft are assumed to be separated by more than the minimum wake-separation distance.
\end{itemize}

Each of these assumptions sits on a step of the study's thread (Table~\ref{tab:se-processes}). The aircraft models supply the truth model that scores every case in step 8 (Section~\ref{sec:scoring-limits}). Security, implementation and accurate reporting are assumptions about the interface chosen in step 7 (Section~\ref{sec:proposed-interface}): the requirements it meets ask for the information to reach the party that decides, not for a means of protecting or checking it. Communication and wake turbulence bound the use case of step 5, and the scenario's representativeness bounds what step 8 can claim.

The explored scenario stands for a narrow set of conflicts: those between two aircraft crossing at the same level. \textcite{rantanen2012conflictManeuvers} found that crossing conflicts with both aircraft in level flight made up only 36 of the 256 cases they examined, maneuvers drawn from 223 conflicts at five U.S. en-route centers. The most common maneuver overall, at 44 percent, was a level-off, which stops an aircraft that is already climbing or descending; the conflicts it resolves involve a level change the scenario does not include, and they offer the controller different options. The scenario is represented through one hypothetical en-route sector and four aircraft, with controllers responsible for maintaining separation and for resolving conflicts. The aircraft are assumed to be at the same altitude and on a collision course. The scenario is designed to be simple enough to allow for a clear analysis of the proposed interface and decision-making process, while still being representative of the types of conflicts that occur in the NAS.

At a minimum, the controllers are assumed to have today's level of information about the aircraft and their intentions. In the baseline scenario, the controllers have only the information that is currently available to them in the NAS. In a proposed improvement, the controllers have access to additional information about the aircraft's performance, which allows them to make more informed decisions about which aircraft to move in a conflict. Inherent in this assumption is that the reported state information is accurate (\cite{kulkarni2019ctopSlotSubstitution} discusses how airlines can game the system with inaccurate information) and that the underlying performance models are sufficiently accurate to support the decision-making process (its simplifications are listed separately in Section~\ref{sec:scoring-limits}).
