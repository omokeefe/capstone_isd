This section presents the identification and formulation of the model the experiment is built on, beginning with the stakeholders, their objectives and the conflict among them. It then identifies the constituent systems through which they act, draws the boundary of the System of Interest, and states the requirements on it. Next it describes the architecture, how a flight operates within it, and where the model shows a gap and the means to close it. Finally, it presents the airline-to-ATC interface whose use informs the decisions that determine the value achieved by the experiment of Section~\ref{sec:experiment}.

\subsubsection{Candidate Objectives}

An exploration of each stakeholder's interests led to the following list of objectives relevant to the project:

Each item is an objective because at least one stakeholder seeks to affect it, and it can be measured, as a measure of performance for one system or a measure of effectiveness for the whole.
\begin{description}
    \item[Safety / Security] Keeping aircraft separated from each other, from terrain and from hazardous weather, and protecting the operation from deliberate harm. Held by every stakeholder; for air traffic control and the flight crew it is a constraint that is never traded.
    \item[Efficiency] Flying the flight with the least fuel and time for the distance covered. Held by the airline, whose fuel cost depends directly on the trajectory flown and whose time cost is determined by the passengers and the number and seniority of the crew conducting the flight.
    \item[Throughput / Capacity] The number of aircraft a sector, runway or airport can handle in a period. Held by air traffic control and the airport authority.
    \item[Reliability / Resilience] The ability of the operation to run as planned and to recover when it is disrupted. Held by the airline, air traffic control, and passengers.
    \item[Environmental Impact] The carbon dioxide, other emissions and contrails a flight produces, which follow from the fuel it burns and the path it flies. Held by environmental and societal organizations and, through regulation, by governments.
    \item[Noise Impact] Sound over the communities near airports, set by the departure and approach paths. Held by communities and the airport authority.
    \item[Passenger Satisfaction] The passenger's experience of travel time, comfort and connections. Held by passengers, and by the airline through the revenue that follows.
    \item[Schedule Reliability] Arriving on time and keeping passengers' connections. Held by the airline.
    \item[Cost / Profitability] The operator's cost of a flight (fuel, crew, the consequences of delay) and the profit it supports. Held by the airline and its investors.
    \item[Maintenance / Lifecycle Cost] The upkeep of aircraft and engines over their life. Held by the operator and the manufacturers.
    \item[Regulatory Compliance] Operating within the rules, procedures and clearances in force. Held by the flight crew and the airline, and enforced by the regulators.
    \item[Workload / Human Factors] The mental and procedural demand on controllers, dispatchers and crews. Held by air traffic control and the flight crew, for whom high workload is a cost and a source of error.
\end{description}

These objectives were then assessed for relevance to the decision under study.

\subsubsection{Selection}
\label{sec:scope}

% Update to use my D-007 language? 
The stakeholders and objectives above are the result of exploration. The study itself is narrower. It examines one tactical decision, the resolution of a conflict between aircraft in cruise, and keeps only the stakeholders and objectives that bear on that decision.

Four stakeholders remain, together with the aircraft, and each appears on the context diagram (Figure~\ref{fig:sysml-systemContext}): operators, as flight operations and its airline operations center; air traffic control, within airspace management; the airport authority, as airport operations; and the flight crew. The aircraft appears as aircraft systems. Governments and standards organizations, aircraft and engine manufacturers, passengers, and environmental and societal organizations are outside the System of Interest. They supply rules, equipment, demand, and expectations to the decision but do not make it or execute it, so they are treated as boundary actors and not decomposed.

Six stakeholder objectives remain: safety, efficiency, environmental impact, schedule reliability, cost, and workload. Schedule reliability is carried in the experiment as a step in cost when a flight's delay passes a threshold (the parameter $K$ of Section~\ref{sec:scoring-method}). The others were set aside for the following reasons:
\begin{itemize}
\item Noise impact: the measure is ``not relevant at cruise.'' 
\item Passenger satisfaction: ``not measurable objectively.''
\item Profitability falls out of the other objectives; it is tracked at the operator's organizational level and is more complex than any one decision.
\item Maintenance and lifecycle cost are largely determined by the number of flight cycles, the chosen takeoff performance, the flight distance and the climb controls, so one air traffic control decision has a negligible effect on them.
\item Security, resilience and regulatory compliance are not changed by the choice between resolution options in the scenario.
\item Throughput and capacity were left for another study. They are also close to ongoing work by the author, and out of an abundance of caution that part of the problem space was avoided.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Objectives and Conflicts}
\label{sec:objectives-results}

Each stakeholder kept in scope pursues a different objective when an aircraft's trajectory is changed. The airline wants the lowest cost for its flights, which combines fuel, time, and the consequences of a late arrival for connecting passengers. Air traffic control wants separation assured with a workload the sector team can sustain (preferrably minimal), and a traffic picture that stays predictable. The flight crew wants instructions the aircraft can fly, given with enough time to set them up.

Some of these objectives align. Burning less fuel generally lowers the airline's cost and lowers carbon dioxide emissions together. Air traffic control's wish for predictability and the airline's wish to keep its schedule both favor aircraft that fly what was planned.

Others conflict, and the conflict this project studies is the first of them: the trajectory that is cheapest for one aircraft is not always the one that costs the sector the least to grant. A request or a resolution that saves fuel for one flight can add instructions, monitoring, and follow-up for the controller. That cost is externalized. It does not appear in the airline's own cost function, and the party that bears it has no means to price it back.

The objectives also operate on different timescales. An airline sets its schedule policy over months, realizes fuel cost per flight, and realizes delay cost in minutes. Air traffic control manages capacity over years through sector design and over seconds through tactical control. A decision made in minutes by one party is judged against objectives the others hold on other clocks.

\subsection{Constituent Systems}
\label{sec:systems-selection}

Stakeholders do not change a trajectory themselves. They act through systems: a controller through the sector's displays and radios, an airline through its operations center and dispatch tools, a crew through the flight deck and the aircraft control mechanisms. And the cost of whatever they decide is actuallized in one place, the aircraft, as fuel burned and time flown. The constituent systems through which the stakeholders act were therefore identified next.

Figure~\ref{fig:sos-exploration} places the NAS at the center and branches outward to the systems that could act on a flight: the aircraft, air traffic control, operators, airports, maintenance suppliers, communication, the regulatory bodies, and passengers. The board is deliberately broad. It follows the system-of-systems view quoted in Section~\ref{sec:introduction}, of many heterogeneous systems at several levels, and attempts to list every system that could bear on a flight before any is set aside. Air traffic control divides into the air navigation service providers, whose terminal and en-route facilities control individual flights, and the national command center, whose traffic management units and flow programs shape demand across many. Operators divide into general aviation, airlines and militaries; within the airline, the operations center and its dispatchers sit beside the business, pilots, ticketing and IT functions.

The board decomposes its branches in two different ways, and the difference reflects the problem the project studies. The organizations that decide, air traffic control and the airline, are broken down to the people who make the decision: the individual controller and the dispatcher. The aircraft is broken down to the physical systems that carry it out: the flight management system's guidance and navigation functions, the propulsion components and the fuel system. The decision about a trajectory is made in one part of the system of systems, and its cost is realized in another.

The aircraft is treated alongside the stakeholders as a constituent system. Figure~\ref{fig:sos-exploration} shows the systems first identified within the NAS, with the aircraft decomposed furthest, as its systems and their performance dictate the cost of each flight.

\subsection{The Gap in the Model and the Means to Close It}
\label{sec:model-gap}

\aidraft{New subsection: steps 7 and 8 of the throughline (the deficiency found in the model; the solution space the model sets out), following the owner's 2026-10-29 chain in \texttt{knowledge/models/experiment-definition.md} \S1 and the reading of D-044. Drafted 2026-11-10 at the owner's request; revised the same day for D-046 (HLR-ATC-08 is baseline) and the reorder.}
This subsection follows one stakeholder need through the model, from the objectives it serves to the exchanges that would have to carry it, and shows where the model finds that it cannot be met. It then shows how the model's own breakdown of the decision sets out the ways to meet it. The gap is not new. The literature reviewed in Section~\ref{sec:litreview} documents it: controllers resolve conflicts by rule~\cite{rantanen2012conflictManeuvers,kirwan2001coraStrategies}, without the aircraft's weight or speed intent, which airlines treat as competitive and do not share~\cite{schultz2012adaptiveClimb}. What the model adds is that the gap can be found from the architecture alone, before any simulation is run, by tracing a written need to the interfaces that would have to carry it. A model of the NAS kept current could find a gap like this one proactively, instead of after studies of controller behavior have explained it.

The thread starts at the airline's objectives of fuel cost, schedule integrity and passenger connections. Stakeholder requirement SN-AIR-05 asks that the NAS take the airline's priorities for a flight into account when that flight's trajectory has to change (Table~\ref{tab:thread-decomposition}). In the model it is refined by the use case \texttt{ResolveEnRouteConflict} (Figure~\ref{fig:use-cases}), which every scheduled flight includes because any flight in cruise may need it. Two system requirements answer it. SLR-CLR-13 is the decision itself: the NAS shall choose, among the maneuvers it can confirm restore separation safely, the one with the lowest estimated impact on the aircraft involved. SLR-INF-02 is what the decision needs: the NAS shall give the party that decides the information needed to preserve safety and pursue optimality.

The use case is carried out by the \texttt{ResolveConflict} activity (Figure~\ref{fig:sysml-resolveConflictActivity}), which sits in air traffic control's monitoring loop in the fly en route phase (Figure~\ref{fig:sysml-flyEnrouteActivity}). The activity names its inputs, which are what the decider can know. Two of them exist today: the surveillance picture, which gives each aircraft's type, level and speed, and the filed flight plans. The third, the airline's performance share, carries the aircraft's state and performance and the airline's priorities, and nothing in the baseline architecture supplies it. The requirements allocate the decision to air traffic control and its decision support, and the information to the airline operations center and the aircraft's flight management system (Appendix~\ref{app:thread-trace}). The party that decides and the parties that hold the data sit in different constituent systems.

The sequence of Figure~\ref{fig:sysml-caseCConflictResolve} lays out the exchanges that carry out the decision, message by message, from the conflict alert to the aircraft rejoining its plan. Every message rides a connection on the context diagram or on an internal block diagram, and all but one of those connections exist today: the clearance and its read-back ride the link between air traffic control and the crew, and the aircraft's state reaches the controller by surveillance. The first message, the performance share from the airline operations center to air traffic control's decision support, has no connection in the baseline. The model holds two exchanges from the airline to air traffic control: flight plan filing, and the traffic flow management link to the Command Center, through which the airline can rank its route options before departure~\cite[p.~4]{kulkarni2019ctopSlotSubstitution}. Neither reaches the sector's decision in cruise, and neither carries the aircraft's weight or the airline's cost of time. The share is drawn in the model as the interface \texttt{airlinePerformanceShare}, tagged as part of the improved architecture and left off the context diagram, which shows the baseline. SLR-INF-02, and HLR-OPS-05 under it, which has flight operations make the share available, are therefore the requirements today's NAS does not meet (Table~\ref{tab:improvement-hlrs}). HLR-ATC-08, which has the decision support use the best information it receives, is met today with what today's interfaces give it: what is missing is the share, not its use. That is the gap, and it is found by reading the model, not by running it.

The model shows that the information does not reach the decider. It does not show what that costs. Case A's choice is worse than it needs to be only if a better option was available and could have been confirmed, and showing that takes the analysis of Section~\ref{sec:experiment}. The model also sets out the ways to close the gap. SLR-CLR-13 breaks down into three HLRs for air traffic control: determine the resolution options it can confirm are safe and can be flown (HLR-ATC-10), determine the impact of each option it considers (HLR-ATC-11), and choose the one with the lowest estimated impact (HLR-ATC-05). Each states what has to be done, not how well. Today's controller meets all three by rule of thumb, so each is tagged as baseline. What changes the quality of the decision is the means used to meet them. The model records four means for these requirements: the controller's rule of thumb, decision support with a low-fidelity performance model, decision support with a high-fidelity model, and the high-fidelity model with the airline's connection priorities. SLR-INF-02 sets what each means needs from outside air traffic control. The rule and the low-fidelity model use only what air traffic control receives today; the high-fidelity means need the airline's share. The four means are the four cases of the experiment (Section~\ref{sec:experiment-method}), and the \texttt{ResolveConflict} activity carries them as its branch on what the controller knows.

Figure~\ref{fig:sysml-analysisParametrics} shows where the model holds the fidelity. The calculations the decision depends on (fuel flow at a cruise condition, the rate of climb left, and whether a level can be held) are declared once, with fixed inputs and outputs. Two usages of the performance model realize them, \texttt{nominalPerformance} and \texttt{actualPerformance}, and they differ only in where the mass comes from: one weight per aircraft type, as today's ground automation assumes~\cite{coppenbarger1999climbPrediction}, or the aircraft's own weight, as the airline's share would carry it. The analysis \texttt{ConflictResolutionScoring} lets each case choose with its own information and scores every choice with one truth model, side by side for each aircraft, each airline, the sector and the system. Changing a case's means changes which usage it reads and what it receives; the architecture does not change. This is the second claim's swap of fidelity with the structure unchanged.

\subsection{Proposed Airline-to-ATC Interface}
\label{sec:proposed-interface}

The items the interface carries are not new. \textcite{coppenbarger1999climbPrediction} listed take-off weight, thrust and drag factors, and the speed profile as candidates for exchange between an airline and air traffic control, and measured what airline-supplied weight does for the prediction of a climb. In Europe, the Extended Projected Profile is sent over ADS-C to downlink the trajectory that the flight management system predicts~\cite{sesarju2019solutionsCatalogue}. What has kept the items out of use is ownership, not feasibility: weight and speed intent ``are considered competitive parameters by airlines and are not available for use''~\cite{schultz2012adaptiveClimb}. A second obstacle is the availability of first-principles data for each aircraft type: thrust, drag and fuel flow. This is the same obstacle, owned by a different stakeholder. The airline guards its weight and speed intent; the performance data for each aircraft type sits with the owners of the performance models and the manufacturers whose data they draw on. The reference model, BADA, cannot be redistributed, which is why open models such as OpenAP are built from open data instead~\cite{sun2020openap}.

The proposal here keeps the concept of information exchange and changes the purpose. In the earlier work the data seeks to make the knowledge of one aircraft's flight path more accurate; here it feeds the decision support of the party that chooses which aircraft to move, thereby providing for asking ``what if'' within a mathematical framework. The interface is typed in the model, tagged as part of the improved architecture, and tied to the requirement it satisfies, so the report can point at a model element where the earlier work has a conceptual finding. Confidentiality is stated as an assumption: the data is disclosed only to the deciding party, and no security mechanism is modeled.

The experiment that follows measures what this interface is worth. Its cases differ only in what the decider knows. In cases A and B the decider knows only what air traffic control knows today from broadcast information -- the aircraft's type, level, speed and route, from which case B assumes a nominal weight. In cases C and D it also has the items this interface carries. The difference between them is scored against the conflict of Section~\ref{sec:objectives-results}, at the level of the aircraft, the airline, the sector and the system.
