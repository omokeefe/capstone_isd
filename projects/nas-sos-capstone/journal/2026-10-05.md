## Session notes — is the optimization experiment worth doing?

The owner opened by questioning the experiment: what value it brings, whether it says anything good about MBSE, and what insight the close examination gives. The stated aim is to show both the value and the cost of MBSE.

### Why the doubt is fair

- The expected effect is small (about 27 gallons and 2.3 minutes per flight in TASAR; negligible en-route benefit in one European validation).
- The value of sharing aircraft data with ATC has been studied already (Coppenbarger, SESAR EPP).
- The simulation compares two ways of operating. It cannot test MBSE as an engineering method, because there is no version of this project done without the model.

### What looks interesting in the 10-04 findings

- Three sources disagree on climb versus descend, each citing fuel, and none uses weight. The controller has the authority but not the weight; the airline and FMS have the weight but not the authority.
- Controllers avoid asking for a climb because they do not know if the aircraft can make it. The missing data removes options, it does not only make the choice worse.
- What goes on the exchange matters: a cost index understates a connection-critical flight, and misreporting it gains little compared with misreporting delay tolerance.
- A small effect can still be a finding. It helps explain why the exchange does not exist.

### A candidate reframing (not decided)

- **Run one check across every decision in the model:** does the actor who makes this decision receive the information needed to make it well?
- **The controller's climb-or-descend decision would be flagged,** because it depends on weight and the controller does not receive weight.
- **If the check finds gaps the owner did not already know, the model found something.** That is a benefit that can be credited to MBSE. If it finds nothing new, the model organized existing knowledge and the claim should be more modest.
- **The four-aircraft simulation then measures one of those gaps.** It becomes one worked example, not the main result.
- **The cost of MBSE is the owner's time,** which the journal and git history record: the context diagram, the D-009 and D-010 rework, and the Cameo/SysML v2 dead end.

Two checks proposed before building anything: a back-of-envelope effect size (does the better choice change with weight inside the 4.5 to 7 percent spread?), and the check done by hand on three or four decisions already in the context diagram.

Open, for the owner to answer from experience: the FMS already computes optimum and maximum altitude. Is the obstacle to sharing them competitive sensitivity, or something else (certification, datalink message sets, nobody owning the requirement)?

### Sheth et al. on hidden airline preferences

The owner found a passage in `seamster2011collabSystems` citing Wolfe et al. (2009) and Sheth et al. (2010): ATC changes flight plans without knowing the airline's preferences, because those are proprietary; preferences rest on ten factors "led by schedule integrity and flight connectivity."

There are two Sheth papers from 2010:

- **Sheth and Gutierrez-Nolasco, ICAS 2010** ("Analysis of Factors for Incorporating User Preferences in Air Traffic Management: A System Perspective"). Read in full this session. A fast-time simulation of one afternoon (18,688 flights). Airlines get a daily budget of "credits" to mark which flights matter most, without saying why. Against first-come, first-served: three route options per flight cut total delay 14 to 30 percent; adding credit priorities cut it up to 42 percent, and 51 percent with about 25 percent more traffic. Priorities with no route options made delay 9 to 17 percent worse. Fairness between airlines (Gini coefficient) barely changed. The authors could not get real airline priorities, so they used a formula from distance, passengers and hub connectivity.
- **Sheth, Gutierrez-Nolasco, Courtney and Smith, AIAA GNC 2010** ("Simulations of Credits Concept with User Input for Collaborative Air Traffic Management," DOI 10.2514/6.2010-8079). This is the one Seamster cites for the ten factors. Five airline dispatchers took part in a simulation at NASA Ames. Not read by the assistant; the owner has since obtained a copy.

Both are about flow management before departure (the AOC ↔ ATCSCC link in the model), not a sector controller resolving a conflict. They support the general claim that the decider lacks the airline's priorities, at a different level from the experiment.

Wolfe et al. (2009) is a book chapter, "A Multi-Agent Simulation of Collaborative Air Traffic Flow Management" (DOI 10.4018/978-1-60566-226-8.ch018). Not read.

### The ten factors

From a table image supplied by the owner. Counts of responses at each score, 17 responses per factor, nobody rated anything below 3. Averages recomputed from the counts.

| Factor | 5s | 4s | 3s | Average |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Schedule integrity | 10 | 5 | 2 | 4.47 |
| Crew connections | 8 | 6 | 3 | 4.29 |
| PAX connections | 7 | 7 | 3 | 4.24 |
| Fuel consumption | 6 | 9 | 2 | 4.24 |
| Delay of flights (more than 5 credits) | 6 | 9 | 2 | 4.24 |
| Avoiding TMIs | 6 | 9 | 2 | 4.24 |
| Number of credits remaining | 4 | 12 | 1 | 4.18 |
| Delay of flights (all) | 4 | 11 | 2 | 4.12 |
| Equipment connections | 5 | 8 | 4 | 4.06 |
| Wind data | 6 | 5 | 6 | 4.00 |

What the table supports:

- **Schedule integrity is clearly first.** It is the only factor where most respondents gave a 5.
- **Crew connections is probably second.** By average it is one rating point ahead of the next group; by count of 5s it is ahead by one or two.
- **The next four are an exact tie on average** (72 points each): passenger connections, fuel, delay of high-credit flights, and avoiding TMIs.
- **Two factors exist only inside the study** ("credits remaining" and "delay of flights with more than 5 credits"), leaving eight real-world factors.
- **Wind data is the most divided** (6, 5, 6).
- **The 1 to 5 ratings are not the scarce credits.** Each respondent rated every factor, so the spread shows agreement, not a budget being spent.

What it means for the experiment: the informed case shares weight and cost index, which mostly carry fuel and a flat value of time. The factor rated highest here, and the connection factors after it, are not carried by either.
