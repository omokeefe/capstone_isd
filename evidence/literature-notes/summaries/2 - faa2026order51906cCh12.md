## What it is

The Airport Compliance Manual chapter that tells FAA regional and district offices how to review a lease between an airport sponsor and an aeronautical tenant. Sections: 12.1 introduction; 12.2 background (rights granted by a lease); 12.3 what a review covers (premises, rights, term, fees, title, subordination, assignment); 12.4 the FAA does not approve leases; 12.5 agreements for aeronautical services to the public; 12.6 agreements covering an entire airport; 12.7 "through-the-fence" access (about half the chapter). Sections 12.8–12.12 are reserved. Companion to Chapter 9 (`faa2026order51906cCh9`).

## Why it's valuable — and to what

One point is usable. The rest is about fixed-base operators, hangars, and access from adjacent land, which is general-aviation material outside the Part 121 scope.

- **Literature review:** none of §2–§4 directly.
- **Decomposition / architecture (§6, §10):** the airport sponsor's authority survives delegation.
  - A lease usually grants three things: use of the airfield "in common with others," the right "to occupy as a tenant and to use certain designated premises exclusively," and "the commercial privilege to offer goods and services to airport users" (12.2a).
  - A review must "ensure the airport sponsor has not entered into an agreement that would surrender its ability to control the airport" (12.3a(2)).
  - A sponsor "may delegate or contract with an entity of its choice to perform any element of airport maintenance or operation. However, such arrangements in no way relieve the airport sponsor of its federal obligations. The airport sponsor has the ultimate responsibility for the management and operation of the airport" (12.6a). A tenant doing the sponsor's job is "a resident agent of the airport sponsor and not … a responsible principal" (12.6c). Examples given: airfield maintenance, collecting landing fees, publishing NOTAMs (12.2c).
- **Stakeholder / objective ontology and RACCI (§7-§9):** 12.6 is a clean FAA statement of **authority staying with one party while execution is done by another.** That is the pattern to-do §7 asks for ("where execution responsibility differs from decision authority"). It supports modeling the airport as the authority over its facilities even where an airline or contractor runs them day to day.
- **Optimization study (§11-§13):** none.
- **Glossary / terminology:** sponsor, signatory/tenant, resident agent, through-the-fence, subordination clause.

## Limits

- **It does not mention gates, terminals, or air carriers.** It does not describe preferential-use or common-use gate allocation, nor who assigns gates day to day. This was already found on 2026-09-28 and is why the chapter was not registered then. The gate-allocation split on `aocAirport` stays **[C]**.
- The 12.6 principle is stated for whole-airport management contracts and maintenance delegation. Applying it to an airline running ramp control or gates at a hub is an inference, not something the chapter says.
- "The FAA does not approve leases, nor does it endorse or become a party to the lease agreements" (12.4), so this chapter cannot tell you what any actual airline lease contains.
