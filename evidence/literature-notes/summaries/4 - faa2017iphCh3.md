## What it is

The FAA's handbook chapter on the arrival phase: descent planning, vertical navigation planning, standard terminal arrival routes (STARs), and descent and speed clearances. Written for pilots, not for one aircraft type.

## Why it's valuable — and to what

- Literature review section: none of §2–§4.
- Decomposition / architecture (§6, §10): how a STAR connects the en-route phase to the approach, and that its design builds in a descent gradient and deceleration segments (p. 3-12).
- Optimization study (§11–§13): the citable, government source for the descent rules of thumb collected in `knowledge/models/b737-max9-rules-of-thumb.md` §2–§3.
- Other: a pilot-side companion to `faa2025aim` on arrivals.

## What was taken from it (2026-10-10)

- "A general rule of thumb for initial IFR descent planning in jets is the 3 to 1 formula. This means that it takes 3 NM to descend 1,000 feet", "based on a normal jet airplane, idle thrust, speed Mach 0.74 to 0.78, and vertical speed of 1,800–2,200 fpm" (p. 3-6).
- "For a tailwind adjustment, add 2 NM for each 10 knots of tailwind. For a headwind adjustment, subtract 2 NM for each 10 knots of headwind." (p. 3-6)
- "To determine ROD for a three-degree path, simply multiply your groundspeed by 5." (p. 3-4)
- "STAR procedures typically include a descent gradient of about 318 ft/NM, or about three degrees." "As a general guideline, deceleration considerations typically add 1 NM of distance for each 10 knots of speed reduction required." (p. 3-12)
- "Descending early results in more flight at low altitudes with increased fuel consumption" (p. 3-3).
- Example slowing-down gates: 250 knots at 25 NM, 200 at 20 NM, 150 at 15 NM (p. 3-4).
